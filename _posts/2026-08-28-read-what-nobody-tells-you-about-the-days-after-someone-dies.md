*A honest guide to the hours, days, and weeks after a loss — what to do, what to let go, and what actually helps.*

Nobody prepares you for the first hour.

Not the grief itself — you expected that, even if you couldn't have imagined its particular weight. What surprises people is everything else. The phone calls that start immediately. The decisions that arrive before the shock has had any time to settle. The strange clarity that descends on some people, making them efficient and functional, followed hours later by a collapse they didn't see coming. The hunger, bizarrely, because bodies continue needing things. The way time moves wrong — too fast in some moments, impossibly slow in others.

And underneath all of it: the question nobody around you seems to be asking. **What am I actually supposed to do right now?**

This essay is an attempt to answer that. Not to tell you how to grieve — grief doesn't take direction — but to tell you what the days after a death actually look like, what demands they make, and what tends to help when nothing feels like enough.

---

## The First Few Days

Survive them. That's the only goal.

There will be phone calls to make — family, close friends, employers, anyone who needs to know. You don't have to make them all yourself. If someone offers to help, hand them the contact list and let them start calling. If nobody offers, ask someone specific: "Can you call these people?" Most people want to help and have no idea how. Giving them a concrete task is a kindness to both of you.

There will be decisions about the body, and they will arrive faster than feels humane. Burial or cremation. Which funeral home. Whether to have a viewing. In many places these decisions have legal timeframes attached, which means they can't be postponed the way you might want to postpone everything. If the person who died left any instructions — written preferences, a conversation you remember, anything — use them. If they didn't, make the most reasonable decision you can with the people around you and accept that there is no perfect answer.

Eat something. This sounds like a platitude. It isn't. The body under acute stress depletes itself rapidly, and making decisions while physically depleted makes everything harder. Accept the food people bring. Order something if they don't. It doesn't need to be a meal. It just needs to be something.

Sleep, if you can. Many people can't, not immediately. That's normal. Don't lie awake berating yourself for not sleeping. Get up, do something quiet, go back to bed. Your body will eventually demand what it needs.

And accept that you will feel multiple contradictory things simultaneously. Relief and grief. Numbness and devastation. The strange inappropriate urge to laugh at something. The guilt about the relief, if relief is part of what you feel. None of these are wrong. They're just grief, which is not one thing and does not behave the way we expect it to.

## The Administrative Avalanche

Nobody warns you about the paperwork.

In the days and weeks after a death, there is an enormous amount of administration that must happen. Death certificates — you'll need more copies than you think, because every institution that needs to be notified requires its own original. Banks, insurance companies, government agencies, pension administrators, mortgage lenders. Each one has its own process, its own required documentation, its own timeline.

You'll need to notify the deceased's employer, if they had one. Cancel subscriptions and automatic payments, ideally before the next billing cycle. Notify any government benefit programs — in many countries these have specific notification requirements and timeframes, and failing to notify them can create repayment obligations. Close or transfer accounts. Potentially sell or transfer property.

None of this needs to happen in the first week. Some of it legally cannot happen until you have death certificates, which take days to arrive. What you can do in the first week is make a list — every institution, every account, every obligation you know about — so that when you're ready to start working through it, you have a map.

If the person who died had an organized estate, an executor, a clear will and accessible documents — this process is still time-consuming, but it's manageable. If they didn't, it's significantly harder, and it's worth knowing that help is available. Estate attorneys can assist with probate. Some banks have bereavement teams with simpler processes. You don't have to figure it all out yourself, and you don't have to figure it out immediately.

Let the first week be what it is. The paperwork will still be there.

## What Actually Helps

Not abstractly. Specifically.

**Accept the concrete offers.** "Let me know if you need anything" is well-meaning and nearly useless. "I'm bringing dinner Tuesday — is 6pm okay?" is actually helpful. When people offer specifically, say yes. When they offer vaguely, convert it: "Actually, it would really help if you could pick up groceries on Thursday." People want to do something. Let them do something concrete.

**Identify the one or two people you can be honest with.** Not the people you have to perform okayness for. The ones you can fall apart in front of, or sit in silence with, or say the strange and difficult things to. Grief is lonely partly because most social situations require us to manage how we present ourselves. You need at least one space where you don't have to manage anything.

**Lower the bar for what counts as functioning.** Got dressed today. Made one phone call. Ate something. These count. The expectation that grief should be followed by resilience and productivity is cultural mythology, not emotional reality. Some days the only accomplishment is getting through the day. That's enough.

**Don't make major decisions if you can avoid it.** The months immediately following a significant loss are not the time to sell a house, change careers, move across the country, or end a relationship. The clarity some people feel after a death can be real, but it's also often grief in disguise — the desire to do something, to create change, to make the pain mean something. Wait. Most major decisions survive waiting.

**Go outside, even briefly.** This also sounds like a platitude. It isn't. The body holds grief, and movement — even just a short walk — changes something physiologically. Not permanently, not dramatically. Just enough to remember that the world is still physical and you're still in it.

## If You're Supporting Someone Who Is Grieving

The most common mistake: waiting to be asked.

Grieving people are often unable to identify what they need, let alone ask for it. The offer of help expires quickly — the first week, everyone shows up. By week three, most people have returned to their own lives, and the person who is grieving is often more lost than they were at the beginning, when the numbness was still doing its protective work.

Show up specifically, and more than once.

Bring food without asking if they want it. Offer to handle one concrete task — pick up prescriptions, take the dog out, sit with them while they make a difficult phone call they've been avoiding. Text or message without expecting a response: "Thinking of you today. No need to reply." Come back the following week. And the week after that.

Do not say: everything happens for a reason. Or: they're in a better place. Or: at least they lived a long life. Or: I know exactly how you feel. These are not comforts. They are ways of ending a conversation you don't know how to be in.

Say instead: I'm so sorry. I don't know what to say but I wanted you to know I'm here. Tell me about them, if you want to. Or say nothing at all and just sit with them.

Grief is not a problem you can fix. The person in front of you doesn't need you to fix it. They need to know they're not alone in it. That's all you're there to do, and it's more than enough.

## Grief Doesn't Have a Schedule

This needs to be said plainly because the cultural mythology around grief is genuinely damaging.

You will not grieve in five stages, moving cleanly from one to the next toward acceptance. That model, derived from observations of the terminally ill and later applied universally, doesn't describe how most people actually experience loss. Grief is not linear. It doubles back. It ambushes you in grocery stores and at traffic lights and six months later when a song plays that you haven't thought about in years.

You will have days that feel almost normal — and then feel guilty about them. Days where you're fine, and then days where the fine suddenly isn't. The first birthday after. The first holiday. The first time you reach for your phone to tell them something before you remember.

There's no timeline on any of it. The people who tell you you should be further along by now are wrong. The people who worry about you because you seemed okay too quickly are also, probably, wrong. Grief is not a performance. It doesn't have to look any particular way to be real.

What is true: if grief is making it impossible to function — not difficult, not painful, impossible — for an extended period, that's worth talking to someone about. Prolonged grief disorder is real. So is complicated grief following traumatic or sudden loss. Getting support from a professional, whether a grief counselor or a therapist familiar with bereavement, is not a sign that your grief is worse than others'. It's a sign that you're taking it seriously.

## Right Now, This Week

You don't need a grief plan. But there are a few things that help even in the earliest days.

Tell two or three people that you need them specifically — not generally, specifically. Give them something concrete to do, even if it's small. Let the food come in. Let the first week be survival, and nothing more.

Make one list: people to notify, accounts to close, things that can't wait. Then put the list somewhere and don't look at it again until you have to. Nothing on it is as urgent as it feels.

Find one person to be fully honest with. Not to perform grief for. To actually be in grief with.

And when well-meaning people say something that doesn't help — because they will, because everyone does — try to hear the care underneath the clumsy words. They don't know what to say. Neither do you. Nobody does. That shared uncertainty is, strangely, one of the things that connects us.

You don't have to know what you're doing. Almost nobody does, the first time.

You just have to get through the day. Then the next one. Then the one after that.

That's the whole job, for now.
